## Article III — The Judicial Branch

**Supreme Court:** nine Justices — but the number is set by **statute**, not the Constitution. FDR's 1937 court-packing plan failed, but the threat shifted the Court's jurisprudence — the famous *"switch in time that saved nine."*

Life tenure during good behavior. Original jurisdiction is narrow; **appellate jurisdiction is where the action is**.

### Judicial review is not in the Constitution

Judicial review — the power to invalidate laws or executive actions inconsistent with the Constitution — is not in the text of Article III. It was **claimed by Chief Justice Marshall in *Marbury v. Madison*** (1803). This is the single most consequential structural development in U.S. constitutional history. Without *Marbury*, the Court is a far weaker branch.

<PullQuote attribution="Marbury v. Madison, 1803">
  It is emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is.
</PullQuote>

### The federal judiciary, at a glance

- **U.S. Supreme Court** — 9 Justices, final appellate authority on federal law
- **U.S. Courts of Appeals** — 13 circuits (11 numbered + DC + Federal)
- **U.S. District Courts** — 94 districts, where federal trials happen

**State courts** handle the overwhelming majority of all U.S. litigation in their own parallel hierarchy. The bulk of contract, tort, family, criminal, and property law in the United States is decided in state courts — federal-court attention is the exception, not the rule.

<Callout>
  Stare decisis — the doctrine of following precedent — is strong but not absolute. *Brown* (1954), *Dobbs* (2022), and *Loper Bright* (2024) all explicitly overruled prior decisions. The Court's willingness to overrule precedent is one of the most important and contested features of the post-2020 era.
</Callout>
