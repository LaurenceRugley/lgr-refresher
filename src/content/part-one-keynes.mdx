## Keynes & Macroeconomic Management

John Maynard Keynes occupies a contested position in this taxonomy. He was not a socialist; he believed in private ownership of the means of production and in markets as the primary allocator of goods. But his *General Theory of Employment, Interest, and Money* (1936) argued that markets can settle at high-unemployment equilibria, that aggregate demand drives short-run output, and that active fiscal and monetary policy is therefore essential to maintain full employment. Keynes is best understood as an *interventionist within capitalism* — the architect of the mid-twentieth-century regulatory-welfare bargain rather than a critic of capitalism's foundations.

### The post-war Keynesian consensus and its breakdown

From roughly 1945 to the early 1970s, advanced economies operated under a Keynesian policy framework: counter-cyclical fiscal policy, fixed exchange rates under Bretton Woods, financial repression, and a tolerance for inflation in pursuit of full employment. The stagflation of the 1970s — simultaneous high inflation and high unemployment — broke the consensus. The monetarist response, led by Milton Friedman, argued that inflation was "always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon" and that central banks should target money-supply growth rather than activist demand management.

The **New Keynesian synthesis** emerging in the 1990s combined Keynesian short-run aggregate-demand reasoning with monetarist long-run discipline: independent inflation-targeting central banks would handle stabilization, while fiscal policy would maintain debt sustainability. This was the operative consensus by 2007.

### Post-2008 revival and the inflation episode of 2021–2023

The Global Financial Crisis broke the assumption that monetary policy alone was sufficient. With nominal interest rates at the zero lower bound, fiscal policy returned to prominence. <Cite id="blanchard-2019" /> argued that when *r* is below *g* (the interest rate is below the growth rate), debt can remain stable with modest primary surpluses — making many advanced-economy debt paths look less alarming than the pre-crisis consensus had suggested. Larry Summers similarly emphasized secular-stagnation dynamics and argued for public investment when the economy is depressed.

The inflation episode of 2021–2023 was awkward for every camp. For mainstream Keynesians, it showed that pandemic-era stimulus was too strong relative to supply constraints. For monetarists and fiscal-theory advocates, it reinforced the claim that large deficits, accommodative money, and delayed tightening produce persistent nominal overshoot. For Modern Monetary Theory advocates, the episode did not falsify the framework — inflation is the real constraint, not a financing constraint — but it did expose the difficulty of calibrating fiscal expansion in real time.

### Modern Monetary Theory and its critics

MMT, associated most prominently with <Cite id="kelton-2020" />, argues that sovereign currency issuers do not face a financing constraint like households or eurozone members: the binding limit on government spending is real capacity and inflation, not "running out of money." Taxes and bond sales are tools for managing inflation and reserves, not for funding spending. In MMT, fiscal policy should be the primary stabilization instrument, with monetary policy in a supporting role.

<Cite id="cochrane-2023" /> and other fiscal-theory-of-the-price-level critics push the opposite emphasis: inflation is ultimately about fiscal backing and credibility. If investors doubt future primary surpluses, inflation or higher borrowing costs will do the adjustment. From this view, debt sustainability is forward-looking — credibility, not debt-to-GDP, is the relevant metric.

<Callout>
  The broadest current consensus is that no framework is sufficient on its own. Keynesianism best explains stabilization in slumps; monetarism and fiscal theory best explain nominal discipline and credibility; MMT best forces economists to distinguish financial from real constraints. The post-COVID synthesis is more pragmatic: use fiscal policy aggressively in downturns, recognize supply-side constraints and inflation risks earlier, and stop treating debt, money, and spending as interchangeable.
</Callout>
