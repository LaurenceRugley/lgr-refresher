## Checks and Balances — The Machine

The bidirectional power flows that make the system work.

<ChecksBalances />

### Congress over Executive

- **Impeachment** of the President or other officers
- **Override of veto** with a two-thirds vote in both chambers
- **Power of the purse** — no funds without appropriation
- **Senate confirmation** of appointments and ratification of treaties
- **Oversight** via committees and subpoena
- **Control of executive branch structure by statute** — Congress creates and can abolish agencies

### Executive over Congress

- **Veto** — including the threat of veto as a negotiating tool
- **Agenda-setting** — through the State of the Union and policy proposals
- **Refusal to enforce** (controversial — see Take Care Clause debates)
- **Negotiation of executive agreements** that bypass treaty ratification

### Judicial over both

- **Judicial review** of legislation and executive action
- **Statutory interpretation** (now without *Chevron* deference, see admin-law section)

### Political over Judicial

- **Senate confirmation** of judicial appointments
- **Impeachment** of judges (rarely successful but constitutionally available)
- **Constitutional amendment** as the ultimate corrective
- **Jurisdiction-stripping** by Congress — Article III §2 gives Congress significant control over appellate jurisdiction (rarely used but real)
- The **long-run effect of the appointment power** — every Supreme Court justice was put there by elected branches

<Callout>
  The point of Madison's design isn't that any one branch is supreme — it's that ambition counterbalances ambition. Each branch has both the means and the incentive to resist encroachment by the others. The system is intentionally inefficient, and that's not a bug.
</Callout>
