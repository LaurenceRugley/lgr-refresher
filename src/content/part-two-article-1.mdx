# American Government

The U.S. constitutional order is built on three Madisonian commitments: **separation of powers** (Montesquieu via Locke), **checks and balances**, and **federalism**. *Federalist* 10 and 51 are the canonical statements — Madison's argument that an extended republic controls faction not by suppressing it but by multiplying and pitting factions against each other.

## Article I — The Legislative Branch

Bicameral by the **Connecticut Compromise** (1787).

- **House:** 435 members, apportioned by population, 2-year terms, initiates revenue bills (Origination Clause).
- **Senate:** 100 members, two per state (the great anti-majoritarian feature), 6-year staggered terms; advice and consent on treaties (2/3) and confirmations (majority); sole power of impeachment trial.

### Enumerated powers and the elastic clause

Enumerated powers in Article I, Section 8: commerce, taxation, spending, declare war, raise armies, regulate currency, post offices, naturalization, intellectual property, etc.

The **Necessary and Proper Clause** — the *"elastic clause"* — is the constitutional hook for most of the expansion of federal authority since *McCulloch v. Maryland* (1819).

### Mechanics worth remembering

- Bills die in committee far more often than on the floor.
- The Senate **filibuster** (requiring 60 votes for cloture on most legislation) is a **rule, not a constitutional requirement**.
- **Reconciliation** is the workaround for budget-impact bills.
- The Speaker of the House controls the floor agenda; the Senate Majority Leader controls calendar but with less unilateral power than the Speaker.
