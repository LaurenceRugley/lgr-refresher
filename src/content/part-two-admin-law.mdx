## The 2024 Administrative-Law Revolution

The 2023–2024 Supreme Court term produced a quartet of decisions that, taken together, constitute the most consequential structural shift in U.S. administrative law since the New Deal settlement. The combined effect is a fundamental rebalancing of *who resolves regulatory disputes* — away from agency autonomy and toward courts, litigants, and, in some settings, the presidency.

<AdminLawQuartet />

### Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo (June 28, 2024)

*Loper Bright* **overruled *Chevron* deference**. The Administrative Procedure Act, the Court held, requires courts to exercise independent judgment about what statutes mean rather than deferring to reasonable agency interpretations of ambiguous statutes.

Existing rules and case law already decided under *Chevron* remain in place from the decision, but the *Chevron* framework — which had been cited roughly **18,000 times in four decades**, making it the most-cited administrative law decision of all time — no longer governs future disputes. Agencies retain only the weaker *Skidmore* form of deference, which courts may give as their own judgment warrants.

### Corner Post v. Federal Reserve (July 1, 2024)

*Corner Post* addressed the statute of limitations for challenging federal regulations under the APA. The Court held 6-3 that the default **six-year clock runs from when a plaintiff is injured by a rule**, not from when the agency issued it.

The decision opens virtually every regulation on the books to challenge by newly affected parties — Justice Jackson, in dissent, warned that combined with *Loper Bright* the ruling would trigger a *"tsunami of lawsuits"* challenging established regulations.

### SEC v. Jarkesy (June 27, 2024)

*Jarkesy* held that the **Seventh Amendment requires jury trials when the SEC seeks civil penalties** for securities fraud, rather than allowing the agency to adjudicate such cases internally through administrative law judges.

The decision sharply limits one important model of administrative adjudication and casts doubt on similar in-house enforcement mechanisms across the regulatory state.

### Trump v. United States (July 1, 2024)

*Trump v. United States* held that former presidents have:

- **Absolute immunity** from criminal prosecution for acts within their core constitutional authority
- **Presumptive immunity** for other official acts within the outer perimeter of their official responsibility
- **No immunity** for unofficial acts

The case did not address administrative law directly, but by reading executive power expansively it strengthened the modern presidency and reinforced the Court's willingness to recenter constitutional gravity around the White House.

### Scholarly assessment: counter-revolution or course correction?

Much of the 2024–2026 commentary describes this pattern as a **"judicial counter-revolution"** against the New Deal administrative order, or at least as the end of the old administrative settlement.

<Cite id="sunstein-2025" /> treats the decision as part of administrative law's *"Grand Narrative"* of judicial reassertion, while warning that practical consequences remain uncertain.

<Cite id="vermeule-2024" /> adopts the language of revolution but argues the Court is revealing a deeper constitutional settlement rather than inventing one.

Among administrative-law conservatives, Philip Hamburger's long-standing position is that the administrative state rests on unconstitutional delegations and a shadowy revival of prerogative power — the recent cases look less like a revolution than a partial recovery of first principles.

Defenders of the administrative state, including <Cite id="metzger-2026" />, characterize the pattern as **"anti-administrativism"**: a coherent project of distrust toward expert agencies that strengthens presidential power while weakening regulatory institutions. <Cite id="desai-2025" /> argues *Loper Bright* is best understood as an *"expressive act"* signaling skepticism toward the administrative state rather than a single knockout blow.

<Callout>
  The best description is that the Court has launched a judicial counter-revolution against the New Deal administrative order — but an *incomplete and uneven one*. It is clearest in judicial supremacy over statutory meaning, broader opportunities to sue old rules, and the constitutional narrowing of agency adjudication. The simultaneous expansion of presidential power means the state is not disappearing so much as being **re-centered around courts and the White House**.
</Callout>
