## Federalism

The **Tenth Amendment** reserves to the states or the people powers not delegated to the federal government.

The historical arc has been one of federal expansion — via the Commerce Clause (*Wickard v. Filburn*, 1942, was the high-water mark), the Civil War Amendments, the New Deal, the Civil Rights era, and the Spending Clause (Congress conditions federal funds on state policy compliance, e.g., the drinking age).

Recent jurisprudence has trimmed at the edges: *U.S. v. Lopez* (1995), *NFIB v. Sebelius* (2012), *Dobbs* (2022) returning abortion regulation to the states. Whether this is a durable trend or a momentary correction is a live debate.

### What the empirical literature finds on federalism

Peer-reviewed political science on federalism since the 2010s has substantially complicated the classic *"laboratories of democracy"* thesis.

<Cite id="garlick-2022" /> finds bottom-up diffusion in many issue areas — state legislative activity predicts subsequent congressional bill introductions, though often via messaging more than implementation. But <Cite id="tyler-gerken-2021" /> characterize "laboratories of democracy" as driven largely by third-party organizations and national networks, not autonomous state problem-solving. Recent work emphasizes partisan learning and growing inter-state divergence tied to party control rather than unbiased experimentation.

### Race to the bottom, top, or middle?

On race-to-the-bottom dynamics, the evidence is **mixed by domain**.

<Cite id="volden-2002" /> found classic AFDC evidence that states did *not* relentlessly undercut each other on welfare benefits — they often wanted to raise benefits but waited for neighbors.

But <Cite id="woods-2021" /> finds that adoption of *"No More Stringent"* laws restricting states from exceeding federal pollution standards is significantly driven by interstate economic competition, providing **direct evidence of environmental race-to-the-bottom pressures**.

Some scholars argue many systems actually show a "race to the middle" — states cluster around similar, mediocre standards due to informational and political incentives. Formal models also show subnational competition can erode central enforcement and reduce overall welfare.

### State-level variation drives major welfare differences

State-level variation in health and welfare policies produces large differences in population health.

<Cite id="montez-2019" /> find that higher minimum wages and state EITCs are linked to lower mortality, better birth outcomes, and reduced disability.

<Cite id="bollyky-2023" /> find that during COVID-19, states with stronger health-care access, more protective mandates, higher vaccination, and higher social trust had substantially lower standardized infection and death rates — without clear losses in GDP or reading scores — though some educational trade-offs (math scores) and employment effects appear.

Legal analyses of Medicaid and ACA implementation by <Cite id="gluck-huberfeld-2018" /> argue that heavy reliance on states produces large, durable inequalities in coverage and access.

<Callout>
  Federalism clearly fosters policy experimentation and diffusion, but learning is often partisan and network-driven rather than technocratic. In some domains — particularly environmental regulation and certain social protections — competitive pressures generate genuine race-to-the-bottom dynamics or at best a race to the middle. State-level variation produces large differences in welfare outcomes; decentralized authority can entrench unequal access to core benefits.
</Callout>
