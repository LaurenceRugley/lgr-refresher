## Communism

Two distinct meanings — conflating them is the source of most confused arguments.

### (1) Communism as Marxist end-state

A stateless, classless, moneyless society where the means of production are held in common and distribution follows the principle *"from each according to his ability, to each according to his needs."* The state has "withered away" (Engels). It is a **post-scarcity moral horizon**, not a governance system. No actual society has claimed to have reached this stage.

### (2) Communism as 20th-century state form

Marxist-Leninist one-party regimes: USSR, PRC under Mao, Vietnam, Cuba, North Korea, the Eastern Bloc. Defining features:

- vanguard party monopolizing political power
- nationalization of the commanding heights
- central planning (Gosplan, the Five-Year Plans)
- suppression of independent civil society

Lenin's *What Is to Be Done?* (1902) is the foundational text on the vanguard party; Stalin operationalized it.

Variants matter:

- **Leninism** — vanguard party
- **Stalinism** — socialism in one country, purges, forced collectivization
- **Maoism** — peasant-led revolution, permanent revolution, Cultural Revolution
- **Trotskyism** — permanent international revolution, opposed to Stalin's bureaucratization
- **Eurocommunism** — 1970s Italian / Spanish / French CPs distancing from Moscow

<Callout>
  Body counts associated with 20th-century communist regimes — documented in *The Black Book of Communism* (French 1997 / English 1999) and successor scholarship — are estimated in the tens of millions: Stalin's purges and the Holodomor; the Great Leap Forward famine; the Cambodian genocide. The debate over how much of this is intrinsic to communist ideology versus to totalitarian political form is ongoing and politically charged.
</Callout>

### The 21st-century Marxist revival

Since the 2008 financial crisis, Marxist and post-Marxist thought has re-entered Anglophone debate in a more public, crisis-driven way. The revival has two distinct wings worth distinguishing.

The **contemporary cultural-theory wing** is the more visible. David Harvey's core claim is that capitalism survives through "accumulation by dispossession," spatial fixes, and periodic crisis management. Wolfgang Streeck shifts the emphasis to the longer decay of capitalism through debt, stagnation, and democratic erosion. Nancy Fraser argues for "cannibal capitalism," depending on the expropriation of not only labor but also care, nature, and social reproduction. Aaron Bastani represents a more journalistic-populist strand, especially around "fully automated luxury communism." Slavoj Žižek remains the most visible public philosopher in the group.

**Analytical Marxism** is a different and intellectually more rigorous tradition. G.A. Cohen, John Roemer, and Erik Olin Wright reconstructed Marxism using analytic philosophy, rational-choice tools, and formal social science, with less emphasis on dialectics or cultural critique. Wright's later work on real utopias connected Marxist critique to institutional design. This wing is closer to political economy and policy debate than to the magazine-circuit version.

<PullQuote attribution="Honest summary of the revival">
  A real but fragmented intellectual revival, with one rigorous academic wing and one much more visible public-intellectual wing — the two overlapping enough that the revival has mattered, even if it has not produced a single new orthodox Marxism.
</PullQuote>

### Marx's textual lineage — a quick map

- ***The Communist Manifesto*** (1848, with Engels) — commissioned by the Communist League. Class struggle as the motor of history; the bourgeoisie's revolutionary role; the predicted polarization between capital and labor. Polemical, not analytical.
- ***Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts of 1844*** — the "young Marx," the alienation argument (labor alienated from product, process, species-being, and other workers). Critical for 20th-century Western Marxism (Lukács, Frankfurt School).
- ***The German Ideology*** (1845–46, with Engels) — historical materialism in its first systematic form. *"Life is not determined by consciousness, but consciousness by life."*
- ***Grundrisse*** (1857–58, notebooks) — method, the "fragment on machines," concepts later refined in *Capital*.
- ***Capital*, Volume I** (1867) — the labor theory of value, the commodity form, fetishism, primitive accumulation, the working day. The only volume Marx published in his lifetime.
- ***Capital*, Volumes II & III** (1885, 1894) — assembled by Engels from Marx's notes. Volume III contains the tendency of the rate of profit to fall, which the "transformation problem" has hung on ever since.
